# Competition and moats

Two diagnostics that feed the Defensibility score. Load this when scoring defensibility or writing a competitive landscape.

---

# Competition topology

**Counting competitors tells you almost nothing.** Five competitors can mean a commodity bloodbath or a category that just got validated and is expanding faster than the incumbents can serve it. Those are opposite situations and a flat penalty treats them the same.

Name the topology. Score the structural opening, not the headcount.

## Empty

Nobody is doing it.

Two explanations, and you must pick one with evidence. Either the enabling condition arrived recently, or there is no demand and others already discovered that. **Default to the second.** An empty market is usually a graveyard, not a gap. Ask what the last three attempts were and why they died. If you cannot find any attempts, that itself is the finding.

## Fragmented

Many small players, none dominant, no consolidation.

Often the best topology to enter. Nobody has the scale to crush you, buyers are used to switching, and there may be room for a rollup or for technology to change the cost base. Look for why it stayed fragmented: local relationships, licensing, or genuinely low returns to scale.

## Concentrated with disliked incumbents

A few players own it and customers resent them.

A real opening, and the one most often overestimated. Dislike is not the same as switching. Measure the friction: contract length, data lock-in, retraining, integration depth, and who inside the customer gets blamed if a migration goes wrong. High dislike plus high friction is a trap that has swallowed a lot of capital.

## Venture knife fight

Multiple funded entrants racing, prices falling, features converging.

Usually unattractive without a structural advantage they cannot buy. Their capital outlasts yours and the category may be commoditized before it matures. Enter only with owned distribution or a niche the funded players will not chase.

## Platform-controlled

The market exists inside somebody else's ecosystem.

Distribution is solved and existential risk is permanent. A policy change ends you. This carries the platform-dependency red flag automatically. Viable, but never as the only channel.

## Winner-take-most

Network effects or scale economics mean one player ends up with the category.

Requires an unusually strong entry thesis. If you are not going to be that player, you are building an acquisition target at best, and more likely a write-off.

# Moat taxonomy

Defensibility is not a vibe. Name the mechanism, then ask whether it grows.

## The types

| Moat | What it is |
|---|---|
| Proprietary data | You accumulate data others cannot get, and it improves the product |
| Workflow lock-in | You become the place the work happens |
| Switching costs | Leaving costs money, time, or risk |
| Network effects | Each user makes it better for the next |
| Regulatory licence | Legal permission others must earn |
| Distribution | You own the route to the buyer |
| Brand and trust | Chosen because of who you are, in a category where being wrong is expensive |
| Cost advantage | You deliver cheaper for a structural reason |
| Scale economies | Unit costs fall as you grow |
| Proprietary supply | You control an input others need |
| Geographic density | Route or coverage density competitors cannot match locally |
| Community | The other members are the product |
| Contracts | Multi-year commitments with real termination cost |
| Intellectual property | Patents or protected work that actually blocks a competitor |
| **None** | A real and frequent answer |

## The question that separates real from decorative

> **Does this moat get stronger as the business grows, or does it stay the same size?**

**Compounding.** Proprietary data, network effects, workflow lock-in, geographic density, community. Every customer makes the next one easier to win and harder to lose. Score 10.

**Static.** Regulatory licence, contracts, brand, cost advantage, proprietary supply. Real, and they hold, but a competitor at your scale faces the same barrier you did rather than a taller one. Score 7.

**Neither.** Reputation, execution quality, a head start, "we care more". Score 4, and be honest that it is a head start rather than a moat.

**None.** Say so and score 1. Plenty of good businesses have no moat and make money anyway. Pretending otherwise costs the reader more than admitting it.

## Two common errors

**Calling switching costs a moat before there is anything to switch from.** Switching costs protect an incumbent. On day one you are not the incumbent, and the switching cost is working against you. Score what protects you *after* you win a customer, and note separately that the same force makes the first sale harder.

**Confusing a hard job with a defended one.** Work that is unpleasant, fiddly, or requires crawling around a roof does deter competitors, and that is worth something. But it deters them equally whenever they decide to try, and it does not deepen. That is static at best.
