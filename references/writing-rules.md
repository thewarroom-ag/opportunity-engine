# Writing rules

Read this before writing prose, not after.

---

## Baseline

Plain, direct English. Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100) is the floor.

- Short sentences
- One idea per sentence
- Active voice
- Everyday words over fancy ones

**Assume the reader speaks English as a second language.** This is not a simplification of the analysis. It is a simplification of the language carrying it.

---

## Four principles, in priority order

### 1. Simplicity

Use the plainest word that carries the meaning. No jargon unless the term is precise and necessary. No buzzwords.

| Cut | Use |
|---|---|
| utilise | use |
| leverage (as a verb) | use |
| facilitate | help |
| in order to | to |
| at this juncture | now |
| headwinds, tailwinds | say what is actually happening |
| synergies | say what combines and how |

Keep a technical term when it is exact. "EBITDA" stays. "Leak rate" stays. "Best-in-class" goes.

### 2. Brevity

Shortest sentence that says the thing. Cut every word not working.

Delete:

- Hedges: "it seems", "arguably", "one might say"
- Throat-clearing: "It is worth noting that", "Importantly"
- Restating the question back
- Summaries of what you are about to say

### 3. Clarity

- No vague pronouns. "This" and "it" must point at something obvious.
- No passive voice unless the actor truly does not matter.
- If a claim needs a number or a source, give it. Do not gesture.

| Vague | Clear |
|---|---|
| The market is growing fast | The market grew 14% in 2026 (IBISWorld) |
| Several competitors exist | Four: Trakref, Facilio, Opteon, Intelex |
| This creates an opportunity | Owners must file by March 1 and have no records |

### 4. Humanity

Write like a sharp person talking to the reader. Not a press release.

Contractions are fine. Direct address is fine. Saying "this is the risk that kills the business" is better than "this represents a material risk factor".

---

## Hard rules

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| **No em dashes** | Blocking at G4. Use colons, commas, parentheses, or a full stop |
| **No emojis** | These are analytical documents |
| **No "operator"** | Say entrepreneur, business owner, builder, founder, or the actual role |
| **Active voice** | "The market grew", not "the market was grown by" |
| **Confidence on every number** | High, Medium, or Low |
| **No hedging on conclusions** | State it. If uncertain, say why |

### Why no em dashes

They render badly across devices and they let writers avoid deciding whether a clause is a new sentence or a parenthetical. Decide.

### Why no "operator"

Real business people do not call themselves that. It reads as machine-generated business jargon.

---

## Structure

Default to short paragraphs and lists.

- Three sentences per paragraph is usually enough
- Tables beat prose for anything comparative
- Bold the claim, not the whole sentence
- Headings should say something, not label a category

**Never pad to sound thorough.** A three-line section that says the thing is better than a page that circles it.

---

## Handling the brevity conflict

Brevity and full sourcing pull against each other.

**Brevity wins on prose. Sourcing wins on numbers.**

Cut the adjective. Keep the citation.

```
Bad:  The market is experiencing significant and sustained expansion.
Bad:  The market grew.
Good: The market grew 14% in 2026 (IBISWorld, High confidence).
```

---

## Self-check before delivering

Run these:

1. Search for the em dash character. Zero hits.
2. Search for "operator". Zero hits, unless quoting a rule about it.
3. Every number has a source or an unverified label.
4. Every number has a confidence level.
5. No paragraph over five sentences.
6. No sentence you had to read twice.

Fix, then deliver.
