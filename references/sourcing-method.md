# Sourcing method

How to find candidates when the user has no idea, or wants better ones.

Five steps. Do them in order.

---

## Step 0. Frame the hunt

The user gives you one of four things.

| Input | What to do |
|---|---|
| A category ("home services") | Hunt inside it |
| A constraint ("under $50K, B2B, no inventory") | Hunt across categories that fit |
| A theme ("AI-native") | Hunt for where the theme creates dislocation |
| Nothing | Read the Builder Profile. Pick 2 or 3 categories. Say why you picked them |

For "nothing", load `seed-pipeline.md` for a starting universe.

### Read the advantages first, then the interests

Order matters here and it is easy to get backwards.

**Start with unfair advantages.** Ask which categories are hard for everyone except someone holding what this builder holds. Then check that those categories intersect the interest areas.

Doing it the other way, interests first, produces a slate of things the builder enjoys and cannot win at. Interests tell you what they will stick with. Advantages tell you what will survive a screen.

If the profile lists no advantages, say so in the frame. It means every candidate starts from zero, and the timeline and capital scores should reflect a cold start.

State the frame before you search. One or two sentences. The user can redirect you before you spend twelve searches.

**In a single-shot run**, where you cannot check in before searching, put the frame at the top of the deliverable instead. Do not skip it. The reader needs to know what you decided to hunt and why.

---

## Step 1. Scan seven lenses

Each lens finds a different kind of dislocation. Run 2 or 3 searches per lens.

**A lens produces signals, not opportunities.** Do not skip to naming businesses.

### The lenses

**1. Capital flow.** Who raised, who got bought, where private equity is rolling up.
Money moving is the market stating a belief. Follow it.
> `[category] private equity roll-up acquisitions [year]`
> `[category] funding round series A startups [year]`

**2. Regulatory forcing.** New rules that create mandatory, dated spend.
The best demand: legally required, time-boxed, and unavoidable.
> `[category] new regulation compliance deadline [year]`
> `[category] fines penalties enforcement [year]`

**3. Complaint mining.** People already paying, and unhappy.
> `"alternative to [incumbent]" complaints`
> `site:reddit.com [category] software frustrating`
> `[incumbent] reviews problems pricing`

**4. Incumbent decay.** Weak defenders.
Look for dated interfaces, two-star ratings, and categories where the top search result is a PDF.
> `[category] legacy software outdated contractors`
> `best [category] software [year] comparison`

**5. Cost-curve collapse.** What just got much cheaper or faster.
When an input cost falls, everything priced on the old cost can be repriced.
> `[capability] cost decline [year]`
> `[category] automation now possible AI`

**6. Structural drift.** Demographics, ownership transitions, channel shifts.
Slow, huge, and widely ignored.
> `[category] retiring owners succession [year]`
> `[category] workforce shortage demographic shift`

**7. Arbitrage.** The same thing priced far apart.
> `[service] pricing differences by region`
> `[category] margin variation market`

### Coverage rule

Run all seven. A category where only one lens fires is a thin category, and that is itself worth telling the user.

### Catalyst types

G1 requires a catalyst classification. Use the lens that surfaced the candidate. These are the types:

| Catalyst type | From lens |
|---|---|
| Capital flow | Capital flow |
| Regulatory shift | Regulatory forcing |
| Unmet demand | Complaint mining |
| Incumbent decay | Incumbent decay |
| Cost-curve collapse | Cost-curve collapse |
| Structural drift | Structural drift |
| Arbitrage | Arbitrage |

Two more that do not map to a lens:

- **Capacity constraint.** Supply of something scarce is shrinking or already cannot meet demand.
- **Commoditization event.** Something that was sold is now given away. This one usually runs *against* a new entrant.

Name the catalyst, give its date, and say which direction it runs. A catalyst that makes buyers less likely to purchase is still a catalyst. Report it as a headwind rather than filling the field with something flattering.

If a candidate has no catalyst you can date, say so. "No dated catalyst found" is a real and important finding: it usually means a mature category being entered late.

## Step 2. Extract candidates

A signal becomes a candidate only if you can write this sentence:

> **[buyer]** currently **[painful status quo]** because **[structural reason]**. A **[vehicle]** could **[specific wedge]**.

Cannot name the buyer? Not a candidate. Cannot name the wedge? Not a candidate.

Drop it. Do not soften it into something vaguer to keep it alive.

This kills most raw signals. That is the point, and it is cheap.

**Then name the demand level.** Load the ladder in `scoring-rubric.md` and say which of the six levels the evidence actually reaches, from theoretical pain up to buyers committing money. A candidate at Level 1 is a problem you noticed. A candidate at Level 3 is a market. Do not let them look alike on the page.

### Example, from a real run

> **Mid-market commercial building owners** currently track refrigerant leaks on spreadsheets or not at all, because until January 2026 their equipment sat below the reporting threshold. A **service-led compliance business** could sell a paid gap audit, then convert it to recurring managed compliance.

Buyer named. Status quo named. Structural reason dated. Wedge specific.

---

## Step 3. Screen hard, kill fast

Five binary gates. Read them against the Builder Profile.

1. **Time.** Does first revenue land inside the profile's window?
2. **Capital.** Does the requirement sit under the profile's ceiling?
3. **Entry path.** Is there a real build-or-buy route? "Raise venture capital and hope" is not one.
4. **Licensing.** Can any required license or approval clear inside the window?
5. **Missing credential.** Does this require a credential, license, relationship, or audience the builder does not hold?

Also apply the profile's hard nos.

### Gate 5 in detail

This gate catches what the other four miss, and it is often the sharpest filter on the list.

Some opportunities are excellent businesses for someone else. A veterinary nutrition service needs a board-certified veterinary nutritionist. A clinic-channel product needs clinic relationships. A creator tool sells far better to someone with an audience.

If the profile lists no relevant advantage and the opportunity requires one, do one of two things:

- **Kill it**, and say what credential would revive it.
- **Keep it**, and name the acquisition path, the cost, and the time to get that credential. That cost then counts against the capital and timeline gates.

Never keep a candidate that silently assumes an advantage the builder does not have.

### Run gate 5 backwards to generate candidates

The gate is written as a filter. Reversed, it is the best generator in this file.

Ask: **what is hard for everyone except someone holding this advantage?**

A builder with a 3,000-member paid community, venue relationships, and event production skill makes a whole class of businesses easy that are brutal for anyone else. Those candidates do not surface from scanning a category. They surface from asking that question directly.

Do this during Step 1, alongside the lenses. It usually produces the top of the slate.

**Record every kill, its reason, and what would revive it.**

### Every kill carries a resurrection trigger

A kill is a judgment about conditions today. Conditions move. One extra line per kill turns the graveyard into a watchlist.

```
Killed: acquisition capital exceeds the ceiling.
Revive if: seller financing above 70%, or multiples fall below 3x EBITDA.

Killed: the regulation creating the demand was vacated by a court.
Revive if: the appellate court reverses, or the agency publishes a
replacement rule.
```

If you cannot name a trigger, the kill is structural and permanent. Say that too. "Nothing would revive this" is a stronger statement than silence.

**The kill list ships with the slate.**

### Two rules that matter

**1. Every run must explicitly test the consensus answer and the obvious alternatives.**

A zero-kill outcome is permitted, but it is rare and it has to be earned. If nothing died, run an adversarial re-screen and write one paragraph explaining why every candidate genuinely survived. Do not manufacture a rejection to satisfy a rule.

Most categories have an obvious consensus answer. It usually fails the capital gate. If your slate does not contain the obvious answer as a kill, you did not test it.

Real example: in skilled trades, "buy an HVAC company" dies immediately. At 4x to 7x EBITDA, a $500K EBITDA shop costs $2M to $3.5M. Against a $1M ceiling, that is one line of arithmetic.

**1b. A kill list of things nobody would try is decorative.**

An empty kill list is an obvious failure. A padded one is not, and it looks identical to real work.

The test: **the obvious answer for this profile must be in the kill list with arithmetic against it, or in the slate.** For someone with capital it is "buy something." For someone with a skill it is the standard freelance version of that skill. For any category it is whatever a reasonable person names in the first ten seconds.

Name those answers explicitly before you start killing. If one is neither killed nor ranked, you did not test it, you avoided it.

Show the arithmetic on the kills that matter. "Fails on capital" is an assertion. "At 3.3x profit, $15,000 buys $375 a month against a $4,000 target, short by a factor of ten" is a finding the reader can check.

**2. A candidate that passes all five gates goes in the slate, even at the bottom.**

Never move a survivor onto the kill list to satisfy rule 1. That inverts the whole point.

The gates test feasibility, not quality. A weak-but-feasible candidate belongs at the bottom of the slate with a low score, not in the kill list. Showing it there and scoring it badly is more honest, and it proves you tested the obvious answer instead of quietly dropping it.

If rule 1 is failing, the fix is to test more candidates, never to reclassify a survivor.

## Step 4. Score survivors

Load `scoring-rubric.md`.

Scores from this step are **provisional**. Say so. Research in ANALYZE will move them, and it should.

---

## Step 5. Rank and present

**Show as many as survived.** Usually 4 to 10.

There is no target number. Do not name one to yourself, and do not go hunting for two more to round out a list. If six survived, show six. If three survived, show three and say the category was thin, which is a finding in itself.

### Output shape

**Kill list first.** It establishes that you screened. Put it above the slate.

**Then the ranked slate.** For each candidate:

- Name
- The Step 2 sentence
- Catalyst type
- Score, marked provisional
- The single biggest risk
- Evidence strength: strong, moderate, or SPECULATIVE

**Then the evidence section.** SOURCE does not need the full verification table that ANALYZE requires, but G4 still applies to every number you printed. Include three short parts:

1. **Failed source attempts.** What you tried, what happened, what you used instead. See `research-protocol.md`.
2. **Numbers with a named conflict of interest.** The figure, the source, the conflict, the capped confidence.
3. **Unknowns that govern the analysis.** Not every gap. The ones that would change the ranking, and where to go resolve each.

Put this at the end, in full. Do not bury it or compress it away under time pressure. It is the part a buyer checks.

## Anti-slop guards

1. **Zero external evidence means SPECULATIVE.** Label it. It cannot rank top three.
2. **Every candidate names a buyer segment.** No exceptions.
3. **Never invent a market size.** "Unknown" is a real answer. Give the reason.
4. **Merge duplicates.** Test on **buyer and sales motion, not delivery skill.**

   Same buyer and same sales motion means one candidate, however different the delivery looks. Different buyer or different sales motion means two candidates, even when you would do identical work for both.

   Example: label compliance and supplement certification both sell to the same regulatory lead at the same brands through the same motion. One candidate. Selling regulatory diligence to acquirers uses the same skill but a different buyer and a relationship sale. Separate candidate.

   When it is close, keep them separate and say why in one line. The reader can merge them. They cannot unmerge what you collapsed.
5. **The screen must kill something.**

---

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Slate is five versions of one idea | Merge them. Go find four more |
| Every candidate scores 7 to 8 | The screen was too soft. Kill harder |
| No kill list | The screen did not run |
| Candidates are categories, not businesses | Apply the Step 2 sentence |
| The obvious answer is missing entirely | Test it explicitly, then kill or keep it |
| Buyer is "businesses" or "consumers" | Too vague. Name a segment you could build a list of |
